McCarthyism and Protecting American Freedoms

What was McCarthyism?

What if you had to leave friends, colleagues, and career, moving across the country to start over with a new job, just because someone told your congressperson that they heard from a guy they met on the street that your spouse was a Communist? This may sound insane, but things like this could have happened during the McCarthyism of the Cold War. McCarthyism was an American ideology during the Cold War that used censorship, blacklisting, government investigations, fear of possible Communists all over America, and fear of being unjustly accused to find “un-American” citizens and Communist spies. It is named after Senator McCarthy because he was one of the most visible proponents of using these tactics for ridding the country of Communists in every form. McCarthy and many others in the American government fed upon the preexisting fears of Communism to create an atmosphere of distrust throughout the country, also known as the “red scare,” using national security as an excuse to restrict people’s constitutional freedoms. The United States government should focus more on protecting the freedom of its people than on national security.

McCarthyism: Fear and Hate.

The American government neglecting the people’s freedoms will divide Americans with distrust, creating a nation divided against itself. Historian Dr. Don E. Carlson states, “The red scare was basically a technique, a tool, a simplistic device for some members of the community to use against a whole set of unwelcome developments threatening those members’ conception of the perfect and proper community. These unwelcome developments usually included the growth of labor unions, the rise of racial and ethnic consciousness, and the penetration of local schools and churches by non-traditional ways of looking at the human condition.” McCarthyism attacked ideas and groups that had no relation with Communists and posed no threat to America or its ideals. Practitioners of McCarthyism used Communism as a label for any ideas they didn’t like, then a decade later, the US entered the Vietnam war to fight Communism, a war that deeply divided the American people. Veteran John Kerry, part of the organization Vietnam Veterans Against the War, testified that “all that they [this administration] have done . . . is to make more clear than ever our own determination to undertake one last mission—to search out and destroy . . . the hate and fear that have driven this country these last ten years and more.” During the Vietnam War the U.S. government spent lots of money and lives (both Vietnamese and American) with the purpose of defeating Communists, but ultimately the president was more focused on winning than on any actual benefit to the people. The government itself feared that they would lose the war, and the people feared the war would never end; hatred for Communists was replaced in some by hatred for the government. The fear of Communism led to people being afraid of their neighbors and coworkers, either that they might be Communist or that those other people might accuse them of being Communists, leading to people having to be careful about what they say.

McCarthyism: Paranoia Over Progress

The American government neglecting the people’s freedoms will stifle Americans’ expression of thoughts and beliefs, preventing new ideas and progress. In 1951, President Truman told members of the American Legion, “The growing practice of character assassination is already curbing free speech and it is threatening all our other freedoms. I daresay there are people here today who have reached the point where they are afraid to explore a new idea. How many of you are afraid to come right out in public and say what you think about a controversial issue? How many of you feel that you must ‘play it safe’ in all things—and on all occasions?” McCarthyism’s witch hunts for Communists often resulted in the casualties of controversial or new ideas because of the overpowering paranoia of being labeled as a Communist or because of the fear that someone else’s ideas might have been sourced from the Soviets. Another peril for these ideas was people who took advantage of the panic to take down anything, or anyone, they disliked by labeling them as Communist. Character assassinations were especially visible in the entertainment industry, and an example of this is screenwriter Jack Moffitt, who (among many similar accusations) wrote to a local congressman about another screenwriter: “[Martin Berkley] IS A COMMUNIST. And, at this present moment, HAS A COMMUNIST CARD IN HIS POCKET. I learned this from an F.B.I. man, who recently resigned from the service.” If someone can get in trouble because some screenwriter claims that a guy who said they used to work for the FBI told them that they saw a Communist card in someone’s pocket, then there is no security. Anyone could make up a story and call someone a Communist and at best they’d be ostracized from their community and at worst investigated by the government; preventing people who have been labeled as Communist from working in their industry means fewer ideas in the industry, and those remaining in the industry may feel afraid they’ll be blacklisted, which may make them play it safe and not try any new and daring ideas.
